# When do particles produced by an expanding shell collide?

The preceding HDblast calculation gives a leading-order quantum energy spectrum for pairs of massless fermions. A bath frequency labels the pair’s total conformal energy. A scattering rate requires single-particle energies, the density of possible targets and an interaction cross section. At leading order the pair spectrum also retains phase correlations. Replacing it by a gas of independent, isotropically distributed scattering partners is a physical assumption. [Earlier HDblast study](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24374531)

## From an emitted spectrum to a collision probability

For an unscattered particle with comoving momentum $k$, a dilute, uncorrelated, homogeneous gas gives $\Gamma(k,t)=\int dn_{\rm target}\,v_{\rm M}\sigma(s)$. For massless incoming particles the flux factor is $v_{\rm M}=1-\cos\theta$, with $\theta$ the angle between their incident momenta. The cumulative optical depth on a specified interval is $\tau(k)=\int\Gamma(k,t)\,dt$. In the independent-collision approximation its survival probability is $e^{-\tau(k)}$. A transport calculation weights the differential cross section by the angular deflection relevant to a particular observable. For example the distinguishable-particle momentum-transfer weight is $1-\cos\theta_{\rm cm}$, where this second angle is the *scattering* angle in the center-of-mass frame. For isotropic scattering this weighted cross section equals the total cross section. Yang and Yu give an explicit differential-scattering example. Lancaster and colleagues use a conformal neutrino opacity $|d\tau/d\eta|=a\Gamma$ and assume a thermal neutrino spectrum when deriving the familiar $T^5$ rate. Oldengott, Rampf and Wong give the quantum gain and loss terms including final-state occupation factors. [Angular weighting, Eq. 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2205.03392#S2.E4) · [Cosmological opacity, Eq. 3](https://arxiv.org/html/1704.06657#S2.E3) · [Scattering visibility](https://arxiv.org/html/1306.1536#S2) · [Boltzmann collision integral, Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/html/1409.1577#S3)

For one distinguishable elastic channel with center-of-mass momentum $k_{\rm cm}$, the partial-wave formula is $\sigma^{\rm el}_\ell=\pi(2\ell+1)|1-S_\ell|^2/k_{\rm cm}^2$. Unitarity gives $|S_\ell|\le 1$. For massless incoming particles $k_{\rm cm}=\sqrt{s}/2$, so one isotropic $s$ wave can contribute at most $16\pi/s$. Bernal, Konar and Show derive the channel normalization for nonidentical scalar particles. Applying this envelope to fermions requires specifying the incident particle and antiparticle channel, allowed helicities and their populations. Additional partial waves, other targets and long-range forces require their own treatment. Saturating the elastic limit at every energy is an upper-envelope calculation. The physical scattering amplitude would have to be specified separately. [Partial-wave elastic and inelastic cross sections, Eqs. 5–7](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.01587#S2.E5)

## What would establish thermalization?

Harigaya and Mukaida studied the formation of a soft bath from energetic decay products. Mukaida and Yamada later solved kinetic equations for a weakly coupled Yang–Mills plasma with redshift, continuous particle injection and nearly collinear splitting. Repeated scatterings can interfere during the formation of a splitting, the Landau–Pomeranchuk–Migdal effect. Their results depend on gauge charge, a screening medium and number-changing processes. [Harigaya and Mukaida, Sec. 4](https://arxiv.org/html/1312.3097#S4) · [Mukaida and Yamada, Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14054#S2)

Two recent preprints illustrate why a collision count alone cannot fix a temperature or a gravitational-wave prediction. Mukaida and Tsuji (September 1, 2026) modeled gravitational waves during perturbative reheating with a hard population losing energy through in-medium splittings and elastic collisions eventually randomizing directions near the thermal scale. Dutta, Ghosh and Khan (October 15, 2026) evolved a nonthermal population of massive scalar particles with elastic self-scattering. Particle number is conserved in their collision term, and their thermalized distribution allows a nonzero chemical potential. HDblast would need a physical scattering model and a calculated approach to an equilibrium spectrum before assigning a temperature to the emitted fermions. A connection to a hot Big Bang would also require a radiation-dominated epoch and an observational test of the proposed origin. [Mukaida and Tsuji, Sec. 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01480#S2.SS2) · [Dutta, Ghosh and Khan, Eq. 9 and Sec. 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.21817#S2.E9)
