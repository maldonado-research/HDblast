# Prior work on heating by a coherent electromagnetic field

The preceding HDblast calculation ends with electromagnetic energy in three synchronized classical fields. It reports a bounce and a brief interval of radiation-like pressure. The outstanding experiment is to introduce a charged population, measure the work done on it and determine whether scattering produces a thermal distribution before contraction resumes. [Published wall simulation](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24387565)

## Current, work and pair production

Gorbar, Momot, Sobol and Vilchinskii evolve a particle distribution in an expanding universe driven by an electric field coupled to a scalar. Their Boltzmann equation distinguishes the Lorentz force, the source of newly created pairs and collisions. Equations (9)–(13) derive the kinetic stress tensor, the conduction current and the work required to create pairs. An oscillating current can retain memory of previous acceleration. Consequently the instantaneous sign of the field work can reverse. Their Eq. (49) models the inverse relaxation time in a weakly coupled relativistic plasma as proportional to the temperature times the fourth power of the effective charge, with a screening logarithm. A seeded classical charge model can test acceleration and current feedback. Starting from an empty distribution also requires a production mechanism. [Gorbar et al., Sec. III](https://arxiv.org/html/1909.10332#S3)

Sobol and colleagues derived quantum kinetic equations for charged scalar pairs in a time-dependent electric field. They renormalized the electric current and stress tensor. The resulting current lags the field in their examples, and the momentum distribution differs from that obtained with a local particle-production rate. An instantaneous conductivity or a count of charged particles alone cannot answer how much field energy returns or what momentum distribution remains. [Sobol et al., Sec. V](https://arxiv.org/html/2004.12664#S5)

## When an energy deposit can be called thermal

Mukaida and Yamada followed particles injected by inflaton decay into a weakly coupled Yang–Mills plasma. Hard particles lose energy through nearly collinear splittings influenced by repeated scattering in the medium. Sections 2.2 and 2.3 specify when the soft sector can be treated as thermal and when the hard splitting rate overtakes expansion. These rates depend on the particle content, occupation and coupling. A chosen drag rate on a wall needs its own microscopic justification. [Mukaida and Yamada](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14054#S2)

A September 2026 preprint by Mukaida and Tsuji makes the distinction visible in momentum space. Under continuous hard-particle injection the approximate distribution can contain a soft thermal component and a hard tail with occupation proportional to momentum to the power −7/2 in their cascade regime. Elastic scattering also randomizes directions on a different timescale. For an HDblast run, comparable mean energy and isotropic pressure can coexist with a nonthermal spectrum. The authors' gravitational-wave spectrum follows from their particular scattering processes. [Mukaida and Tsuji, Sec. 4 and Eq. (4.1)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.01480#S4.E1)

A quantitative equilibrium check requires the particle distribution or a collision law with a known equilibrium. For example, Cho, Hwang, Lee and Yun constructed a relativistic relaxation model for a reactive gas mixture, imposed the conservation laws and proved an entropy theorem with a common-temperature Jüttner equilibrium and chemical potentials obeying the law of mass action. These properties come from their specific operator. A positive drag-heating term in a fluid model measures modeled dissipation. Its energy density alone cannot establish a thermal spectrum. [Cho et al., Sec. 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02037#S3)

The relativistic ideal-gas closure of Mathews used for the tilted charged fluids also has a kinetic interpretation. Mignone and McKinney's Eq. (13) gives the specific enthalpy as $h=(5/2)\Theta+\sqrt{1+(9/4)\Theta^2}$ with $\Theta=p/\rho$ in units with $c=1$. It saturates Taub’s enthalpy inequality and interpolates between the nonrelativistic and ultrarelativistic limits. Using this equation in a fluid solver supplies a rest-frame pressure for specified internal energy. Testing equilibration calls for additional information about the distribution. [Mignone and McKinney, Sec. 2.2](https://arxiv.org/html/0704.1679#S2.E13)

For a collisionless charged gas the Lorentz-force flow preserves phase-space volume on the relativistic mass shell. An external field can broaden or displace a momentum distribution while conserving its fine-grained entropy in a closed population. One distribution-level diagnostic for smooth classical data is the relative entropy to the Jüttner distribution with matching particle number, momentum and energy. It equals the equilibrium entropy minus the actual entropy because the logarithm of the equilibrium distribution is a linear combination of those collision invariants. Cold delta-function beams need a finite initial spread or an explicitly reported coarse-graining before using that diagnostic. [Sarbach and Zannias, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.3532)

## Earlier Maldonado research

The D-Blast 3 archive's January 13 closure note and September 8 research handoff tie a proposed pulsar-timing feature to assumptions about curvature, relativistic particle content and the redshift to the LISA band. Those temperature and frequency mappings are conditional. Assigning them to the present simulation would require a physical scale, a matter sector and an expansion history connecting it to observations. [Public January closure release](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18236838)

A reusable method from the author's DMDE project is to retain separate contributions from the charged-lepton and neutrino channels and test that omitting one changes the result. Here the analogous controls distinguish field work, directed kinetic energy, dissipated energy, expansion work and any pair-rest-energy source. DMDE's nuclear and weak rates apply to its own particle distributions and cannot calibrate the new wall carriers. [DMDE methods release](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22399940)

The literature search prioritized kinetic calculations of time-dependent currents and distribution-level tests of thermalization. The portfolio review used the public Zenodo creator index, the current HDblast release, public DMDE and AntiMatter project descriptions, and the two D-Blast 3 notes above. These sources do not supply an independently calibrated wall charge, carrier abundance or scattering time.
