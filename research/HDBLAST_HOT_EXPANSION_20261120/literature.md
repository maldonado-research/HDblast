# What can sustain a hot, expanding closed wall?

## The competition between radiation and curvature

Binétruy, Deffayet, Ellwanger and Langlois derive the cosmological junction equation for a homogeneous brane in a five-dimensional bulk. It includes the square of the total surface density, an integration constant divided by the fourth power of the radius, and the spatial-curvature term. Splitting the surface density into tension and additional matter gives both a linear and a quadratic matter term. For conserved radiation, density falls as the fourth power of inverse radius. Its linear contribution to the expansion rate squared then falls as the fourth power and its high-energy quadratic contribution as the eighth power. Positive spatial curvature contributes a negative term falling only as the second power. These scalings explain why an initially radiation-dominated closed wall can recollapse. A pulse that supplies more energy can also change the gravitational field. In a different five-dimensional model of an irradiated closed brane, Keresztes, Képíró and Gergely found competing effects from outward radiation pressure and increased self-gravity after absorption. Applying their balance to a new pulse requires its own junction dynamics. [Binétruy et al., generalized Friedmann equation](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/9910219) · [Keresztes et al., Sec. IV](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/0603223)

## Coupling a rolling pseudoscalar to electromagnetism

The interaction density proportional to θ(ψ) Fμν F̃μν is independent of the metric after inclusion of the spacetime volume factor. For a constant θ it is a total derivative in the Abelian theory and gives no local classical force. With a rolling scalar, its energy transfer is proportional to θ′(ψ) ψ̇ E·B. The signed work enters the scalar and electromagnetic energy balances with opposite signs. The transfer can reverse as the field phase changes. Cuissa and Figueroa derive the equations and build a lattice discretization preserving the gauge and shift symmetries of their linear coupling. Adshead and colleagues find that gauge-field amplification during axion preheating depends strongly on coupling and backreaction. In their theory vacuum fluctuations seed the modes. Here a finite classical field is already present. In a runaway-axion model, Hashiba, Kamada and Nakatsuka found that including Schwinger-produced charged particles could substantially modify the field amplification. They explicitly treated that backreaction with an approximate effective instability parameter. A charged wall needs its own microscopic production and current law to quantify this effect. [Hashiba et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10822) · [Cuissa and Figueroa, Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.03132#S2) · [Adshead et al., Sec. 6](https://arxiv.org/html/1502.06506#S6)

Campeti and colleagues obtain a finite period of gauge production from transient motion of a spectator axion in specified potentials. This supports testing a limited interval of energy exchange. For the present wall, choosing a smooth θ′(ψ) concentrated in a particular part of the roll supplies a new interaction profile. Its location, width and normalization need independent physical justification. Taking θ′ to zero, reversing its sign, and shifting its support relative to E·B can distinguish energy drawn from the scalar from redistribution of the original pulse. An integrated energy balance tests this distinction even while individual field components grow. A claim of helicity generation would also require measurements resolving the two helicities. [Campeti et al., Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.03401#S2)

The wall equations give a simple bound on both scalar–field couplings. Define the comoving electromagnetic energy $U=a^4 K(X^2+B^2)/2$ for the electric and magnetic amplitudes $X,B$, with $K>0$. Where $U>0$, let $c=(B^2-X^2)/(B^2+X^2)$ and $s=-2BX/(B^2+X^2)$. These satisfy $c^2+s^2=1$. The scalar contribution to the electromagnetic power in the wall convention therefore obeys

$$
P_{\rm scalar}=\frac{U}{K}(\dot K c+\dot\theta s),
\qquad |P_{\rm scalar}|\le\frac{U}{K}\sqrt{\dot K^2+\dot\theta^2}.
$$

For an isolated mode whose only energy exchange is with the scalar, integrating gives $|\log(U_f/U_i)|\le\int\sqrt{dK^2+d\theta^2}/K$. In the coupled wall, incident and outgoing waves and electric work on particles also change $U$. The pointwise power bound still applies to its scalar contribution. It explains how a large coupling step can produce little signed work if the electromagnetic phase is unfavorable.

A second check is a prescribed, instantaneous scalar step at fixed radius. For bounded incident fields and currents and positive finite endpoint couplings, the magnetic field is continuous. Integrating the Maxwell equation through the step gives $K_f X_f=K_i X_i-\Delta\theta B_i$. Thus $U_f/U_i$ ranges over the eigenvalues of the positive quadratic form $[(K_i X_i-\Delta\theta B_i)^2+K_f^2 B_i^2]/[K_iK_f(X_i^2+B_i^2)]$. Its normalized matrix has determinant one. Its minimum and maximum eigenvalues are $e^{-d}$ and $e^d$, with
$$
\cosh d=1+\frac{(K_f-K_i)^2+(\Delta\theta)^2}{2K_iK_f}.
$$
For example, if $K_i=K_f=K$ and $\Delta\theta=K$, different incident phases give ratios from $(3-\sqrt5)/2$ to $(3+\sqrt5)/2$. The limiting jump prescribes the scalar trajectory. The finite-width cosmological model also evolves its backreaction and may transfer less energy or reverse the transfer. For initially pure magnetic energy, the jump gives the same final electromagnetic energy as an even coupling alone ending at $K_{\rm eff}=K_f+(\Delta\theta)^2/K_f$. The jump with nonzero $\Delta\theta$ gives electric fraction $(\Delta\theta)^2/[K_f^2+(\Delta\theta)^2]$, so the charged particles encounter different fields despite equal total energy. For the primary exit $K_f=6000.04$ and $\Delta\theta=28000$, this expression gives 95.6097% electric energy. On the resolved trajectory the electric share is 0.00313% at entry and 95.6334% at exit. The pure-magnetic jump prediction differs from the resolved exit by 0.0237 percentage points. These comparisons follow directly from the wall Maxwell equation. ## Distinguishing particle energy, radiation pressure and temperature

In a weakly coupled gauge plasma fed by energetic particles, Mukaida and Yamada calculate how in-medium splitting supplies energy to a soft bath. Mukaida and Tsuji show that continuous injection can leave a hard power-law tail while softer modes are approximately thermal. Nearly collinear splitting can transfer energy down the momentum distribution while preserving its direction. Elastic scattering changes directions on a different timescale. Consequently, tracking the duration of high particle energy and pressure near one third of energy density tests expansion and stress. Establishing a common temperature also requires the species and momentum distributions and independently justified collision rates. [Mukaida and Yamada, Sec. 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.14054#S2) · [Mukaida and Tsuji, Eq. (2.31)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01480)

The D-Blast 3 September shell study already found quick recollapse after rolling toward two modified zero-energy minima. The reported maximum ratios of radius to bounce radius were 1.25 and 2.06. The interval began at the bounce, so neither number measures growth after a hot state formed. Its reheating-temperature estimates explicitly used a dimensional decay width with an undetermined prefactor. The November neutral-parent study instead measured the short interval with a high daughter internal-energy fraction and derived the hypothetical matching radius needed for later radiation expansion. Those two clocks and their different matter models are important when comparing a new radius doubling with the earlier work. [September dynamics report](https://github.com/maldonado-research/HDblast/blob/add30482b0aba462518c062f119bfa991f3d038e/hdblast/checkpoints/2026-09-16_chat09_shell-dynamics/HDBLAST_CHAT9_SHELL_DYNAMICS_20260916/blast_dynamics/BLAST_DYNAMICS_REPORT.md) · [November neutral-parent study](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24406309)

As an observational example, Barbieri and colleagues evolve incomplete neutrino thermalization in a specific late-reheating cosmology and report a 95% lower bound of 5.96 MeV for the reheating temperature in their analysis. Akita and colleagues' October 2026 preprint studies reheaters with different masses and decay channels. Energetic injected neutrinos can change the neutron-to-proton ratio even when bulk energy observables look similar. A temperature in MeV or a primordial abundance from HDblast would require a physical normalization, particle content, weak rates and a time history connecting the wall calculation to the observed universe. [Barbieri et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01369) · [Akita et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36062)
