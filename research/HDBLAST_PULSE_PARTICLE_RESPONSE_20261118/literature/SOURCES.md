# What a massive quantum probe can measure

The B1 matter prescription activates at a scalar-field threshold. A free quantum probe with a smoothly varying mass obeys a different equation. Primary calculations establish that its state can change while its frequency stays positive throughout. They also give exact examples in which a nonzero pulse leaves the final occupation at zero. Neither outcome can be inferred from whether the threshold was reached.

### Two exact comparisons

Alves and Camilo solve a scalar mass quench in flat spacetime with positive-frequency plane-wave states at both ends. Their Eq. (21) interpolates the squared mass with a hyperbolic tangent of width δ. Using Eqs. (25)–(26) and writing ωᵢ and ωₒ for the initial and final mode frequencies gives

$$
|\beta_k|^2=\frac{\sinh^2[\pi\delta(\omega_o-\omega_i)/2]}
{\sinh(\pi\delta\omega_i)\sinh(\pi\delta\omega_o)}.
$$

Both endpoint masses can be strictly positive. A finite change in mass then gives a nonzero asymptotic occupation for each finite momentum. The paper also derives a smooth pulse that returns to its original mass in Eqs. (28)–(32). The D-Blast 3 September analysis adapted that example to a constant positive asymptotic mass, obtaining an exact zero at integer pulse index. The B1 experiment replaces the prescribed pulse shape with histories drawn from the shell evolution. [Alves and Camilo, arXiv:1712.01400](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01400) · [Project provenance](PORTFOLIO.md)

Repeated disturbances combine coherently. For an elementary check, take an oscillator of positive frequency ω, raise its frequency to Ω for time T, then restore ω. Continuity of the mode and its first derivative at the two jumps gives

$$
|\beta|^2=\frac{(\Omega^2-\omega^2)^2}{4\Omega^2\omega^2}\sin^2(\Omega T).
$$

For ω = 1 and Ω = 2, this is 9/16 at T = π/4 and zero at T = π/2. This per-mode check assumes instantaneous jumps and makes no claim about the ultraviolet energy of a field. In an independent smooth example, Dabrowski and Dunne show that two electric pulses yield constructive or destructive interference depending on momentum. Their Figs. 7–8 compare the plateau between pulses with the final result. The propagation phase matters even when individual disturbances have the same strength. [Dabrowski and Dunne, arXiv:1405.0302](https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0302)

### The rolling-shell comparison and its short-crossing assumption

Felder, Kofman and Linde derive the instant-preheating spectrum for a scalar passing through a zero of the coupled particle mass. For zero bare mass its generalized occupation is $n_k=\exp[-\pi k_{\rm phys}^2/(g|\dot\phi_0|)]$ and its integrated density is $(g|\dot\phi_0|)^{3/2}/(8\pi^3)$. The derivation approximates the background by $\phi(t)\simeq\dot\phi_0(t-t_0)$ through the production region. A disturbance that changes the velocity within the formation time tests this specific premise. The derivative correction in the HDblast event rule is a further project assumption. The rolling study propagates the modes across the simulated event to check the approximation. [Felder, Kofman and Linde, *Instant Preheating*, Eqs. (2)–(4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9812289) · [Rolling-shell equations and comparison](../classical/ROLLING.md)

### What the shell calculation can distinguish

Dabrowski and Dunne use the same evolved state with two choices of positive-frequency basis. Their Eqs. (13)–(14) and Figs. 1–2 show that the choices yield different transient populations and agree at an asymptotically adiabatic endpoint in their examples. Namba and Suzuki formulate the Bogoliubov coefficients directly between specified asymptotic bases using mode inner products and a conserved Wronskian in Eq. (V.16). Cembranos, Garay and Parra-López choose an initial de Sitter vacuum and prescribe either an exit to a static universe or an adiabatic out prescription for a massive conformally coupled scalar. These choices supply physical information beyond a measured finite segment of shell history. A stationary extension of the pulse provides a repeatable in/out measurement whose result belongs to that extension. [Dabrowski and Dunne](https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0302) · [Namba and Suzuki, arXiv:2509.19194 (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19194) · [Cembranos, Garay and Parra-López, arXiv:2602.04970 (2026), §§2.2 and 3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04970)

Louko and Satz give the leading response of a switched two-level detector in Eq. (2.3), a double integral of the Wightman two-point function along its worldline weighted by both switch values and the phase from the detector energy gap. It can be nonzero in the vacuum for finite switching. A matched pulse-minus-quiet comparison of the same switch, initial state, gap and momentum window measures a change in this response. A restricted momentum window specifies only that band's contribution to the full local detector response. [Louko and Satz, arXiv:0710.5671](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.5671)

Habib, Molina-París and Mottola give the scalar energy balance in an expanding background with constant mass in Eq. (4.8). Their Eqs. (4.9)–(4.10) exhibit the occupation, vacuum term and phase correlations entering energy and pressure before renormalization. With a prescribed mass squared that depends on the shell scalar, the corresponding work balance is

$$
\dot\rho_\chi+3H(\rho_\chi+p_\chi)
=\tfrac12\,\frac{d m_\chi^2}{d\tau}\,\langle\chi^2\rangle_{\rm ren}.
$$

Here τ is proper time. This extension follows from varying the external mass term in the scalar action and requires consistent subtractions in the stress and the source. A closed model must include the compensating loss by the field supplying the work. The finite-band response leaves this energy budget, interactions with visible species, and thermalization open. [Habib, Molina-París and Mottola, arXiv:gr-qc/9906120](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9906120)
